# Reviewing an interface against Apple's Human Interface Guidelines

A procedure for checking an app (native, Flutter, React Native, Tauri or Electron) against Apple's [Human Interface Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/) (HIG): five lenses, a severity scale, a report format and special review modes, with a map from each design topic to the HIG page and to the rule or skill of this repository that governs it. The method is adapted, as concepts only and in our own words, from [dickwu/apple-design-skill](https://github.com/dickwu/apple-design-skill) (read 2026-11-05). That repository has no licence file and bundles 123 copied HIG pages (Apple's text, which belongs to Apple Inc.), so nothing is copied or vendored here: no page text, no tables, no scripts.

Apple's HIG pages are rendered with JavaScript and their text could not be read with the tools used for this review. Where this file mentions a guideline detail it is labelled "per the third-party summary" and must be checked on Apple's page before it goes into a finding, with the date you read it ([03-verification](../../../rules/03-verification.md)). The pages show a "last change" date; record it, because guidance such as the Liquid Glass material was updated recently. Apple's App Review Guidelines page, by contrast, was readable and is quoted below where used.

## 1. Method

Review as two people at once: a platform reviewer who knows the guidelines, who keeps the design honest against the platform, and a design lead who keeps it from looking like every other app. Every review carries both.

1. **Set the context.** Platform and framework; the app's category and audience; the artifact (screenshots, mockups, wireframes, code or a description) and what it can and cannot prove; the design's thesis (one sentence: what is the single job of this screen, and what is most characteristic about it); and what the owner wants (a full audit, a worry, a direction). Infer what you can and ask only when the answer changes the verdict. A web or Android-only app gets the principles and the foundations (accessibility, colour, type, layout, writing) but not Apple's platform conventions: say which parts apply. Contrast and sizes need real values from the design or the code, never an estimate from a compressed image; mark any estimate as one.
2. **Read before you cite.** Open the HIG pages that match what is on screen (the topic map below), about eight to twelve per review, not the whole set: always accessibility, layout, typography and colour, the page for the platform (iOS, iPadOS or macOS), and a few for the components shown. Quote the guideline and name the page and heading. If no page covers a point, say it is your judgement. Apple changes pages; do not review from memory.
3. **Filter by the principles first.** A screen that breaks a principle has a bigger problem than any single guideline it breaks. Per the third-party summary, Apple names eight: purpose (what is it for), agency (can people explore, skip and recover), responsibility (are permissions and data use transparent), familiarity (patterns match the platform), flexibility (sizes, inputs, text sizes, abilities), simplicity (every element earned its place), craft (spacing, alignment, wording, animation finished) and delight (a feeling, the right one, not decoration).
4. **Audit through five lenses, in this order** (section 2).
5. **Report** (section 3) and test on devices and with accessibility settings (VoiceOver, Dynamic Type, Reduce Motion, Reduce Transparency, Increase Contrast) ([accessibility-audit](../../accessibility-audit/SKILL.md)).

## 2. The five lenses

1. **Accessibility (failures are Critical).** Text scales with the system setting and layouts survive the largest sizes with hierarchy intact; contrast is computed from real values and shown as numbers; target size and spacing meet the platform minimum (per the third-party summary, 44 by 44 pt is the mobile default and a smaller minimum exists, with desktop figures lower; check the Accessibility page, and WCAG [10-accessibility](../../../rules/10-accessibility.md) as the floor); nothing is conveyed by colour alone; every icon-only control has a text label for screen readers; desktop works by keyboard alone; motion is optional and never the only carrier of meaning; reduced motion, reduced transparency and increased contrast each have an answer.
2. **Platform conventions (failures are usually High).** Mobile: top-level navigation in a tab bar (tabs navigate, they do not act; few tabs; none hidden or disabled; short labels); actions on the current view in a toolbar, the key one prominent and the rest quiet; important search in a primary position; one sheet at a time with a way out besides Done; content inside safe areas and within one-handed reach; swipe back and row swipe actions work. Desktop: every command reachable from the menu bar; standard shortcuts respected; windows resize fluidly and use system controls; sidebars shallow and hideable; settings under the app menu; pointer feedback on everything interactive. Both: light and dark appearance without an app-specific switch, semantic colours, and translucency only on the floating functional layer, never in content ([apple-cross-platform-and-glass](apple-cross-platform-and-glass.md)). All of these are per the third-party summary of the pages in the topic map: verify each on the page.
3. **Visual design and craft (findings are High or Medium).** One colour means one thing and works in light, dark and increased contrast; few typefaces and a clear scale, with weight and size carrying hierarchy; alignment and grouping that show hierarchy, progressive disclosure over density, layout that follows the space available rather than the device; icons that share one visual language and match adjacent text weight; motion that is purposeful, brief, cancellable and rare on frequent actions. Then the craft questions: does it have a point of view (name the one thing it would be remembered by; a deliberately quiet utility can be the right answer, say so); is it a template (a palette, pairing or layout that arrives with no reason rooted in the product is a default, see the dated tells in [ui-patterns](../../anti-slop-filter/references/ui-patterns.md)); does the typography carry personality where it should (system type for navigation and controls, brand in display text and defining moments); does structure encode something true (numbering, eyebrows and dividers say something about the content); is the boldness spent in one place; and what accessory could go without loss.
4. **Interaction (findings are usually Medium).** Something appears immediately while loading, people can keep working, progress is determinate when possible; feedback lives in the interface, not in alerts; alerts are rare, direct, never on launch, never for common undoable actions; destructive, irreversible actions get a warning and a Cancel, and undo covers the rest; modal views have an obvious way out and one short task; data entry pulls from the system, offers choices over typing and validates as people go.
5. **Content and writing (findings are usually Medium).** A label says what happens ("Save changes", not "Submit") and keeps its name through the flow; capitalisation follows the platform and is consistent on a screen; errors say what went wrong and how to fix it without apologising; empty screens invite the next action; names come from what people control and recognise, not from how the system is built; no jargon or filler ([14-ux-writing-and-ethics](../../../rules/14-ux-writing-and-ethics.md)).

**At home versus unmistakable.** The tension between "feels native" and "could not be mistaken for anyone else" is real. Resolve it by letting familiar components carry navigation and controls, with a restyled component keeping familiar size, placement and behaviour, and putting identity into colour, type, imagery, tone of voice and a few defining moments ([project-design-identity](../../project-design-identity/SKILL.md)). Brand colour belongs on primary actions and status, or in the content layer; logos do not repeat (the Branding page).

## 3. Severity, rating and report

Tag every finding: **Critical** (accessibility failures, unusable on some devices or sizes, conventions broken in ways that confuse, anything that blocks release), **High** (real friction, poor contrast or readability, looks foreign on its platform, a template with no point of view), **Medium** (suboptimal patterns, missed system components, small inconsistencies), **Low** (polish and edge cases). The summary rating follows from the tags: critical issues when any Critical exists; needs work when several High findings exist; good when nothing is Critical and at most a couple of High remain; excellent when nothing is above Medium and the craft lens found a point of view. A design with no Critical or High findings gets a short report: the summary, what works, a few improvements.

Each finding gives where (screen, or file and line for code), the HIG page and heading with the quoted sentence and the date read, what the design does, the fix and its severity. Say which findings come from a design file or a screenshot, which from code, and which rest on a page you could not open. A limit of the artifact is a limit, not a finding.

### Special review modes

- **App icon**: layered composition, clear edges, centred content, no text unless essential, no replica of the interface, dark and tinted variants built from the light icon (pages `app-icons`, `icons`).
- **Accessibility audit**: walk lens 1, then screen-reader order and labels, keyboard-only paths, Switch Control, captions, and visual or haptic doubles for audio cues (`accessibility`, `voiceover`).
- **Dark mode**: semantic colours, softened whites, both appearances tested, icons and images checked, no app-level appearance toggle (`dark-mode`, `color`, `materials`).
- **Liquid Glass or any translucent surface**: the checklist in [apple-cross-platform-and-glass](apple-cross-platform-and-glass.md).
- **Apple Pay and payment marks**: section 4 of [apple-cross-platform-and-glass](apple-cross-platform-and-glass.md); a failure is Critical because it blocks release.

## 5. Cross-platform cautions

- A Flutter or React Native app that imitates iOS controls on Android, or Material controls on iOS, feels wrong on one platform. Use the platform's own navigation and sheets where the framework provides them, and match the platform for system transitions.
- Do not use Apple's marks, product names or Apple Pay artwork except as the brand guidelines allow; link the official asset pages rather than redrawing them.
- App Review can reject for design problems and for misleading branding. Treat the review guidelines as a separate document to read at the time of submission ([release checklist](../SKILL.md) section 6).
- Glass and translucency over content are legibility risks. Measure contrast over the real background in every theme, and honour the system's reduce-transparency and increase-contrast settings.
