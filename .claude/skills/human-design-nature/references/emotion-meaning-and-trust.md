# Emotion, meaning and trust

How feeling, brand character and credibility enter design decisions. Cited as [E-nn] ([evidence-register](evidence-register.md)).

## 1. Decide the feeling before the style

- Affect has two axes: pleasant to unpleasant (valence) and activated to calm (arousal) [E-11, B]. Name the target as a point on them. A health-record app for worried parents wants pleasant and calm; a sale countdown wants activated, and a dark pattern abuses exactly that ([14-ux-writing-and-ethics](../../../rules/14-ux-writing-and-ethics.md)).
- Match the moment, not only the product: a payment failure is not the time for a playful tone. The same product can be calm in an error state and lively at a milestone.
- Three levels of response [E-10, C]: the look (visceral), the use (behavioural), and what it says about the person (reflective). A design can win the first and lose the second; judge all three.

## 2. Brand personality, as a vocabulary

- A usable set of five dimensions: sincerity, excitement, competence, sophistication, ruggedness [E-09, B]. Use it to choose two or three words for the identity and to test whether type, colour, imagery and voice agree with them.
- The set is not universal. Japan and Spain showed shared and local dimensions (peacefulness; passion) [E-09]. Outside the US, ask the target users how they describe comparable brands before you rely on the five.
- Personality words drive the identity brief ([project-design-identity](../../project-design-identity/SKILL.md)). They are inputs to a choice, not proof that the choice works.

## 3. Colour carries feeling, mostly the same way and partly not

- Across 30 countries colour-emotion associations were largely shared (average similarity .88) and shaped further by language and geography [E-08, B]. It was word association, not screen behaviour. So: choose hue by the intended feeling, then check meanings in the specific market with local people. Never treat a colour table as a rule.
- Colour never carries meaning alone: about 1 in 12 men have colour vision deficiency [E-18, A]; pair colour with text, icon or position (WCAG [E-26, A]).
- Saturation and colourfulness are matters of group taste that differ by country, age, gender and education [E-07, B]: a reason to test with the real audience, not to assume.

## 4. Trust

- In a large 2002 study people most often commented on visual design when judging credibility, more so for finance sites [E-14, C; news coverage only]. Read it as: looks are noticed first and quickly [E-02]. Do not read it as: looks earn trust by themselves.
- What turns a first impression into trust is content that is real and checkable, working controls, honest copy and consistent behaviour. This is what [anti-slop-filter](../../anti-slop-filter/SKILL.md) enforces. A dark pattern or an invented testimonial costs more than any visual polish gains.

## 5. Shape and other conventions

- One study found curved contours liked more than sharp ones, with 14 participants and everyday objects [E-13, C]. That is far too thin to say round buttons are friendlier. Treat shape language (sharp, soft, round) as a convention to be chosen to fit the personality words and then tested with the audience.
