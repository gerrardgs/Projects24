# Perception and cognition: how people take in a screen

What the registry supports about seeing, judging and processing an interface. Entries are cited as [E-nn] ([evidence-register](evidence-register.md)). Strength letters are in the register; `A` is a standard or an undisputed finding, `B` is credible research with limits, `C` is a lens.

## 1. People group before they read

- Elements that are near each other, alike, moving together, enclosed in a common region or aligned along a line are seen as belonging together, before any label is read [E-01, A]. Use spacing, alignment and a shared region to show structure; a border around everything is a weaker signal than space.
- Consequence for tokens: spacing steps carry meaning. If the gap between a label and its field equals the gap between two fields, the form groups wrongly.

## 2. The first impression is fast, and it is about looks

- Visual appeal is rated reliably after about 50 ms [E-02, B]. That tells you the first screen composition is judged almost instantly. It does not tell you the product is trusted, usable or worth paying for; do not cite it for those.
- In screenshots of 119 real sites, low visual complexity combined with high prototypicality scored best; complexity is read first, prototypicality counts more as viewing time grows [E-03, B]. Practical reading: for products people must trust or operate, spend the novelty budget on one signature element, keep structure familiar.

## 3. Familiar, but not identical

- Preference rises with novelty while typicality is not lost; the balance depends on the category [E-04, B]. A bank and a music festival should not land on the same point of that trade-off.
- Easy-to-process stimuli feel better [E-05, B], and repeated exposure increases liking [E-06, B]. Together: consistent navigation, labels and component behaviour across screens is not dull, it is what lets people relax into the product. Break consistency only for a reason you can say aloud.

## 4. Ease of use and looks are tied together, up to a point

- People call attractive things easier to use, and forgive small problems in them, but not large ones [E-15, B]. Two consequences. A polished prototype can hide real problems in a usability test, so test tasks, not impressions. And polish cannot repair a flow that fails.
- Beauty and hedonic quality stay stable with use, while perceived usability and "goodness" change as people hit problems [E-12, B]. The first-use delight must be backed by day-thirty usability.

## 5. Affordances are not a style choice

- Weak clickability cues cost time and attention: 22 percent more time and 25 percent more fixations in one controlled study [E-16, B]. A flat or minimal look is allowed when controls still look and read as controls (the interface gates of [anti-slop-filter](../../anti-slop-filter/SKILL.md)).

## 6. What follows for the work

| Situation | Do |
| :--- | :--- |
| New product, structure unknown | Start from the category's familiar structure, differentiate with colour, type, voice and one signature element |
| Dense tool used all day | Low complexity, familiar patterns, consistent behaviour; expression through precise detail |
| Campaign or portfolio | Raise novelty and complexity on purpose, and still keep controls legible |
| A flow is failing | Fix the flow; do not add polish |

## 7. Not used, deliberately

Laws and numbers that circulate widely (Hick's law, Fitts's law, "7 plus or minus 2", golden-ratio layouts) are not in the register because their primary papers were not checked in this review. They may be right; cite them only after adding an entry.
